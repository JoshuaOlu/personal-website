Dear Fola,

**Subject: “Re: I’m Here, Barely, But Here”**

I’m writing this in pieces. A paragraph today. Two sentences tomorrow. My husband, Michael, has been reading your emails to me for the past three years. Yours is the only one I asked him to read aloud. The only one that wasn’t “work.”

It’s taken me five days to write this. I have to stop frequently. Rest. Come back. But I wanted you to know I’m here. Barely, but here.

I got sick in December 2020. COVID. I thought I recovered by February, but I didn’t. What came after was worse than the virus itself. I had Brain fog so thick I couldn’t read a full paragraph. Fatigue so profound that showering felt like climbing a mountain. Crashes that left me bedridden for days after just an hour of mental work.

They call it **Long COVID**. Some call it ME/CFS (Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome). The names don’t matter much. What matters is that my brain, the instrument I’ve relied on my entire career, became unreliable. Untrustworthy. Sometimes just... absent.

Michael became my full-time caretaker. He read emails to me when I couldn’t look at screens. He wrote responses I dictated in fragments. He learned to recognize when I was about to crash and would guide me to rest before I pushed too far.

Your emails (all six of them) he read those to me multiple times.

When your first three arrived in 2020, I was drowning in my new role at CGEEP, and I mistakenly buried them in a folder. When I finally found them in June 2020 and responded, I promised I’d keep reading. Then I got sick.

Michael read your fourth email to me in early 2021. I was in the thick of the fog then. I remember crying (or trying to). Even crying took energy I didn’t have. You wrote about EndSARS, about Chidi, about becoming complicit in everything. I wanted to respond. I couldn’t.

He read your fifth email in late 2022. You’d lost your job for refusing to sign a false safety report. I dictated a response to Michael three different times. None of them were coherent. I gave up.

Then, a few months ago, he read your sixth email. The one about Amidat. The one where you said you finally understood why I couldn’t give you answers.

Fola, I don’t know if you believe in timing, but that email arrived exactly when I needed it.

**I’ve been on a slow path to recovery since early 2023**. It’s not linear. I still crash. I still have days where my brain feels like static. But I have more good days now. Enough that I can write this myself, slowly, in pieces.

Your sixth email did something unexpected. It reminded me why I went into this field in the first place.

I didn’t become a professor because I wanted to maintain the status quo. I became one because I genuinely believed (naively, perhaps) that I could help democratize engineering. That I could make it more accessible, more just, more human.

But somewhere along the way, I got absorbed into the machine. CGEEP. Grants. Publications. Partnerships with the very corporations whose licensing models create the problems you’ve been writing to me about. I saw the contradictions clearly, but I told myself that incremental change within the system was better than no change at all.

**Then I got sick.** And from the fringes (because that’s where chronic illness puts you, on the fringes of everything) I started seeing things differently.

You wrote something in your sixth email that I can’t stop thinking about: **“Perhaps this is what** **mentorship truly is, not the transfer of answers, but the inheritance of difficult questions.”**

You’re right, Fola. And you’ve taught me something I couldn’t have learned from a position of health and institutional stability.

There’s a Greek myth I’ve been thinking about a lot during my illness (whenI have the strength to think). Not Sisyphus this time, but Philoctetes.

Philoctetes was a great archer, one of the heroes sailing to Troy. But on the way, he was bitten by a snake. The wound became infected, foul-smelling, and wouldn’t heal. His cries of pain were so loud that the other Greeks couldn’t stand to be near him. So they abandoned him on an island (Lemnos) for ten years.

He survived alone. In pain. With a wound that wouldn’t close.

But here’s the thing: the Greeks eventually realized they couldn’t win the war without him. Without his bow, without his skill, Troy would never fall. So they came back for him.

The myth asks a question that has no clean answer: **What do you owe to a system that abandoned** **you when you became inconvenient?**

Philoctetes eventually agreed to help them. Not because they deserved it. Not because they apologized adequately. But because he decided that even in his brokenness, even from the fringes, he could still do something that mattered.

I think you’re Philoctetes, Fola.

The system (academia, industry, licensing structures, all of it) abandoned you when you became inconvenient. When you insisted on ethics. When you refused to pretend. When you tried to use LibreSim. When you wouldn’t sign a false safety report.

But you didn’t stay abandoned. You built something from the fringes. Fifteen students in a mentorship program. A year of teaching. Amidat writing to you with the same questions you once wrote to me.

You’re building your own bow. And you’re teaching others how to build theirs.

I don’t know if the system deserves what you’re doing. I don’t know if CGEEP or Microris University or any of the institutions that failed you deserve the engineers you’re training. But I know that Amidat deserves you. And the next fifteen students will too.

From where I sit now (on the fringes myself, learning to live in a body that no longer performs the way I need it to) I can see something I couldn’t see before.

The center is not where change happens. The center is where the status quo perpetuates itself. Change happens on the margins. In the spaces where people who’ve been pushed out start building something different because they have no choice.

You asked me in your first email: “What do you do when the ethical choice is impossible?”

I told you I didn’t know. I still don’t. But I think you’re showing me the answer: **You build** **alternatives.** Slowly. Imperfectly. From the fringes.

You teach Amidat even though you can’t promise her a clean path. You mentor fifteen students even though you can’t guarantee they won’t face the same impossible choices you did. You keep building your bow.

And maybe (this is what I’m learning from my illness) maybe that’s enough. Maybe we don’t need to fix the whole system before we act. Maybe we just need to tend our own small gardens and trust that others are tending theirs.

**I can’t promise you that I’ll be able to respond again, Fola.** I’m learning to live within my limitations now. Some days I can think clearly. Other days I can’t. I’m learning not to push past what my body can give.

But I wanted you to know: **I’ve been here, in the silence, listening.** Michael has been reading your words to me. And they’ve mattered more than any conference paper, any grant proposal, any strategic plan I ever worked on.

**You’ve reminded me what this work was supposed to be about.**

Thank you for not giving up on me. Thank you for understanding, even before I explained, why I couldn’t respond. Thank you for teaching me about mentorship, about building from the fringes, about tending trees when you can’t see the forest.

I’m proud of you. I’m proud to have witnessed your journey, even from this distance.

Keep planting, Fola. Keep teaching. Keep building your bow.

And if you write again (even if I can’t respond) know that Michael will read it to me. And I’ll be listening.

With deep respect and gratitude,

**Patricia**

Patricia Wilkerson, Ph.D.  
Professor of Engineering Education  
Director (on medical leave), Center for Global Engineering Education and Policy  
University of Etthinghton

P.S. You mentioned that you came across my promotion to full Professor. The process started before I fell ill, but the announcement happened right after I got sick, while I was bedridden. It felt important at the time. Now it feels like something that happened to someone else, in another life. What feels important now is this: that Amidat wrote to you. That you’re sitting with her question the way I sat with yours. That the conversation continues, even when individuals like me can’t hold up our end of it. That’s the work that matters. That’s the legacy.
