Dear Prof. Wilkerson,

I don’t know what to say.

I’ve been sitting here for three hours trying to find words, and they keep failing me. I read your email four times. Then **I saw the picture Michael attached of you both in your garden, and I** **cried**. I cried like I haven’t cried since I was six years old, sitting in my mother’s lap after scraping my knee so badly I thought I would never walk again.

I don’t know why the picture undid me the way it did. Maybe because you were real in a way you’d never been before. Not Dr. Wilkerson from the conference. Not the professor whose keynote changed my life. Just Patricia. A woman in a garden. Smiling, but tired. Very tired. Leaning slightly on Michael. Alive, but barely. Just like you said.

**I’m sorry you’ve been suffering**. I’m sorry the thing that gave you time to read my letters also took so much from you. I’m sorry that I kept writing into your silence, not knowing you were fighting just to think clearly enough to remember your own name some days.

But I’m also grateful. More grateful than I know how to express.

Your email about Philoctetes has been sitting with me these past few days. I keep thinking about wounds that won’t close. About being abandoned on an island. About still having something to offer from the fringes.

I think I finally understand something about ethics that I couldn’t see before.

**Ethics is not a luxury**, like you said in your keynote. But it’s also not a set of rules we follow when it’s convenient. Ethics is something innately human. It’s the thing that makes us sit with Amidat’s question instead of brushing it aside. It’s the thing that made you respond to a random Nigerian student even though you were drowning in emails. It’s the thing that makes us refuse to sign false reports even when it costs us our jobs.

**Ethics is the wound that won’t close. The thing that keeps hurting because we’re still alive** **enough to feel it.**

I told you in my last email that I didn’t know how to respond to Amidat. That I was sitting with her letter the way you sat with mine.

I’ve replied to her now. But I couldn’t use a letter. I had to schedule a call with her.

We spoke for two hours last week. I told her about SolidAxis and the three 2007 licenses. I told her about the accreditation lie. I told her about LibreSim and the humiliation. I told her about the false safety report and losing my job. I told her about the fog that came after, the drifting, the shame.

And then I told her what you taught me: that she’s standing in an aporia, and there is no clean path forward. That whatever she chooses will cost her something. That ethics in a broken system looks less like following rules and more like choosing which fractures you can live with.

But I also told her something I didn’t know when I first wrote to you.

I told her that **she doesn’t have to choose alone.** That there are fifteen other students in the mentorship program facing the same impossible choices. That there’s a growing community of engineers across Africa trying to build alternatives. That she can use the pirated software to pass her courses and still learn the open-source tools for her future. That surviving and building don’t have to be mutually exclusive.

I told her that ethics isn’t about purity. It’s about direction. It’s about asking, every day, **“Am I** **moving toward the person I want to become, or away from them?”** And some days the answer will be “away,” and that’s okay, as long as you notice. As long as you don’t stop asking.

She wanted to cry on that call. I think she felt seen. I could hear it in her voice. And then she said something I wasn’t expecting: **“Thank you for not lying to me.”**

That’s when I understood why you couldn’t give me simple answers five years ago. Because simple answers would have been a lie. And you cared about me too much to lie.

I’m receiving my next cohort of students in two weeks. Twenty-three have signed up this time. Word is spreading. And I’ve been thinking about how to structure the program differently.

Before, I taught open-source tools mostly as a practical alternative. Cheaper. Accessible. A way to avoid piracy. And all of that is true. But it’s not enough.

**I want to teach ethics as the philosophy behind the tools, not just cost or convenience.** I want to teach that choosing HopenCAD over pirated SolidAxis isn’t just about legality. It’s about building a different kind of engineering practice. One that doesn’t depend on systems designed to exclude us. One that we can pass on to the next generation without shame.

I want to teach that **every tool we choose is an ethical choice**. That every line of code, every simulation, every design decision carries the weight of who we’re becoming.

I don’t know if I’ll succeed. I don’t know if twenty-three students choosing open-source tools will change anything at a systemic level. But I know that Amidat needed that phone call. And maybe these twenty-three students need something I can give them too.

You wrote that change happens on the margins. I think you’re right. The center will keep  
perpetuating itself. But out here, on the edges, we’re building something different. Slowly. Imperfectly. One student at a time.

The system as a whole may not be getting much better. But **my corner is getting brighter.** And for me, I think that is enough.

Prof. Wilkerson, **I want you to know that I won’t write again**. Not because I don’t want to, but because I don’t want to add to the cognitive pressure you’re managing. **You’ve given me more** **than I had any right to ask for**. Five years of witness. Two responses that changed how I see everything. And now, this final letter that taught me what it means to keep building even when your body is breaking.

**I’ll be praying for you.** For your recovery. For more good days than bad days. For Michael, who has carried you through the worst of it. And for the moment when you can look at your garden without exhaustion, just peace.

If you recover enough to write again, I’ll be here. But I won’t expect it. And I won’t burden you with more letters.

**Thank you, Professor. Thank you for seeing me when I was invisible.** Thank you for naming the aporia. Thank you for teaching me about Procrustes and Philoctetes and the courage it takes to build from the fringes.

**Thank you for showing me that ethics is not about being perfect.** It’s about being human. And refusing to stop being human even when the system demands it.

I’m planting trees, Professor. And some of them are starting to grow.

With deep gratitude and respect,

Fola Adeoluwa  
Mechatronics Engineer  
Founder, OpenPath Engineering Mentorship
