Dear Dr. Wilkerson,

**Subject: “I Kept My Resolution, But I Lost My Job”**

Happy New Year.

It’s been a while since I last wrote to you, and quietly, I had been hoping you would write back, the way you said you would in your last response. Still, even without that, I find myself writing again tonight with mixed feelings. I lost my job today. I was fired. I don’t yet know how to feel about it.

A lot has happened since my last email, so let me slow down and give you some context.

Life had been mostly good for me. I graduated shortly after my last email to you, with a first class. After that, I completed the compulsory one-year National Youth Service. When I finished, I got a job about four months ago.

At the beginning of last year, I made a quiet resolution to myself. I told myself that I would no longer bow to the compromises of using pirated software in industry. I knew what that meant. It meant that many design and simulation roles here in Nigeria were automatically off the table for me.

I remember one interview in particular. It was a small automation startup. At the end of the interview, when they asked if I had any questions, I asked whether the company used licensed software. They couldn’t give me a clear answer. When the offer letter came, I declined it.

Eventually, I settled into a role as a junior automation and maintenance engineer in a fairly established indigenous manufacturing company. I am not yet registered with COREN, but the role was a graduate engineer position and I already developed some skill with automation during my 6- month 400 level internship, so I was accepted.

At first, everything felt normal.

I worked on troubleshooting PLC-controlled machines, mostly older systems. I assisted technicians during breakdowns. I handled sensors, relays, contactors. I wrote maintenance notes. For the first time, I felt like I was finally practicing what I studied.

My manager liked my work. He told me I was sharp. That I understood the machines.

Then, quietly, the first line was crossed.

One afternoon, a filling machine began tripping intermittently. After tracing the fault, I realized the issue was a faulty proximity sensor that sometimes failed to detect bottle presence. The proper fix was simple but expensive. The sensor needed replacement, and it would take time to arrive.

Production did not want downtime.

My manager told me to bypass the sensor temporarily so the machine could keep running. He said it would be replaced later. I hesitated, but I complied.

The machine ran. Operators intervened manually more than usual. Nothing happened.

A month later, the ethical problem became more obvious.

The company was preparing for a client audit. I was handed a document titled “Machine Safety and Preventive Maintenance Compliance Report.” My name was already typed under “Prepared by.”

The report stated that all safety interlocks were functional, that all sensors were operational, and that preventive maintenance was conducted weekly.

I knew this was not true.

The proximity sensor was still bypassed. Two emergency stop buttons did not work. Preventive maintenance logs had been copied from the previous year.

I raised my concerns carefully but my manager sighed and lowered his voice. He told me that I was not COREN-registered, that he was, and that the report was his responsibility. Then he said the sentence that has stayed with me since.

“Just sign. This is how industry works.”

When I pushed again, he asked me if I wanted to keep my job. He told me that everyone already knew how these things worked. That this is Nigeria.

He also said that if I did not sign, he would get someone else to do it.

I went home unsettled.

Two days later, during a night shift, a bottle jammed on that same machine. An operator reached in to adjust it but the machine moved unexpectedly because the sensor was bypassed. Nothing happened, thank God. But it was not because the system was safe, the operator was just really lucky. He could have lost that hand.

The next morning, management told us not to write an incident report.

That afternoon, I was again asked to sign the audit document.

This time, the consequences for the bypass and inadequate maintenance was no longer abstract to me. I could picture a person getting injured. I could picture my name on a document that said everything was fine.

I refused to sign.

I tried to be respectful. I said I could not put my name on something I knew was not accurate. But of course, it all turned on me.

From that point on, everything changed quietly.

I was told I was not a team player. I was excluded from meetings. My probation period was suddenly mentioned often. Two weeks later, today, I was told that my services were no longer required.

There was no query or anything leading to it, we literally just resumed for the new year on Monday, and today, Thursday, I was served the letter.

Just like that, it was over.

I kept my resolution, Dr. Wilkerson. I did not lie. I did not sign. I did not pretend.

But, I lost my job.

I keep thinking about what you wrote to me about aporia, about being trapped in impossible choices. This felt exactly like that. I was told I had no authority, yet I was expected to carry responsibility. I was told ethics mattered, until they became inconvenient. I was told safety was important, until it cost production.

I don’t feel proud tonight. I also don’t feel regret. I mostly feel tired. I’m just really really tired.

I don’t know what this means for my future in this profession. I don’t know how many times a person can choose integrity and still survive. But I know that if I had signed that document, something in me would have broken quietly.

Honestly, I’m not sure how many times I can handle brokenness before becoming permanently marred.

Thank you for teaching me the language to name what I am experiencing, even if the language does not make it easier to live with.

**Warm regards,**  
**Fola Adeoluwa**
