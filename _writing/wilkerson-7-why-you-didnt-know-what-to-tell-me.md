Dear Dr. Wilkerson,

**Subject: “I Now Know Why You Didn’t Know What to Tell Me”**

I hope this email finds you well.

The last time I wrote, I had just lost my job for refusing to sign a false safety report. I told you I kept my resolution but lost my job. I told you I was tired.

I am writing again although this time not from a place of immediate crisis. Nothing has collapsed today (at least, not yet). Nothing has been taken from me. If anything, life has been audibly quiet.

It has been about fourteen months since my last letter to you.

For a long time after losing my job, I did not really recover in any meaningful sense. I did not really find another role. I did not reinvent myself. I drifted more than I would like to admit. I woke up late many times, postponed applications, convinced myself that I was “thinking things through,” but actually I was mostly tired and a little ashamed.

About a month into that fog, an old friend reached out to me. He was organizing a short online engineering tutorial over a weekend and needed someone to teach solid modelling. He wanted me to take it and suggested I use a pirated copy of a popular proprietary CAD package and then went further to mention that I might need to show participants how to pirate the software as well.

I declined. Not angrily. Not righteously. I just told him I would not do that. I added, almost as an afterthought, that I could teach the same ideas using a free and open-source alternative.

He hesitated but I think he was on a short timeline and would go with almost anything so he agreed.

The course ran for a single weekend. It was a paid course. I charged for my time (of course). I needed to survive, and I have stopped pretending that survival is a moral failure. Still, something unexpected happened during those sessions. As I taught solid modelling using the free software, I felt a clarity I had not felt in months. I was working (kinda), but I wasn’t defending myself. I was not refusing anything. I was simply building, and helping others build.

That weekend did not solve my life, but it did something quieter. It reminded me that I still belonged to my discipline. And it gave me hope. Hope that I probably won’t have to struggle forever.

Since then, I have been teaching online regularly. I focus mostly on teaching free and open-source engineering tools, online, to people who want to learn. For example, last month, I taught a weekend course on HopenCAD to twelve people across four states. One participant was a final-year student who couldn't afford SolidAxis. Another was a young engineer who'd just been fired from her job for refusing to use pirated software. (I understood her situation very well.)

My classes are usually small in size. I think the highest I’ve had so far in a single class is 52. The fees are also not that high, but they’re enough to keep me afloat.

Eventually, I had this idea to start a one-year mentorship program for students and early stage graduates that want to master the free and open-source software in their field. Fifteen students signed up and now, we’re three months in.

**I am writing to you today because I got an email.**

It was from one of my mentees, a third-year mechanical engineering student named Amidat.

She wrote to thank me for the classes and mentorship, but that was not the full intent of her letter. **She said she felt like she was living two lives.** In school, she pirates software because, as she put it, “that is the only way to pass.” Outside of class, she is deliberately learning free and open-source tools because she hopes to start her own company someday and does not want her professional life to be built on theft.

She said she feels dishonest in both directions. Sometimes, she feels too ethical for school and at other times, she feels too compromised for her own future.

As I read her words, I felt an uncomfortable recognition. I remembered my initial letters to you.

It wasn’t just because her situation was identical to mine, but because the shape of the problem was still the same. She is in this same system that demands results without providing legitimate means. A quiet insistence that everyone already knows this is how things work. A moral cost that is real, but never officially acknowledged.

For the first time, I understood something about our correspondence that I had not before.

When I first wrote to you, I wanted answers. I wanted principles that would resolve my confusion. I wanted to know which choice was correct. I now realize that what I was asking for was not advice, but relief.

And you could not give it.

I am now in your former position, holding someone else’s question, and finding that I do not know what to tell her either. I can describe the system honestly. I can validate her discomfort. I can refuse to lie to her. But I cannot hand her a clean solution that does not cost her something real.

I wanted to tell her: ‘Use the free software and accept the academic consequences.’ But I remember my LibreSim humiliation. I wanted to tell her: ‘Just pirate what you need to survive.’ But I remember the shame I felt writing to you about it.

So I understand now why you couldn’t give me simple answers. There are none.

This has been a sobering realization.

It is one thing to refuse to sign a document and bear the consequences by myself. Its another thing to look at a young student and explain that integrity does not always protect you, that compromise is sometimes structurally enforced, and that adulthood often consists of choosing which fracture you can live with.

I have not replied to Amidat yet. I am sitting with her letter, the way you once sat with mine.

Perhaps this is what mentorship truly is, not the transfer of answers, but the inheritance of difficult questions.

I wanted you to know that, wherever you are, your silence taught me something your words could not have. It taught me the weight of responsibility that comes with being taken seriously.

Thank you for that, even if I only understand it now.

And I wanted to also congratulate you on becoming a full Professor two years ago. I’m sorry, I just came across it while trying to look for other ways to contact you. I noticed that you’ve not been traveling for conferences and you’ve not been active anywhere online since 2020. Your last email said you’d read if I wrote back. It’s been almost three years since then. I hope the silence means you’re busy, not that something is wrong.

I hope you’re fine, Dr. Wilkerson.

Thank you for the opportunity to keep writing these letters to you.

**Warm regards,**  
**Fola Adeoluwa**
