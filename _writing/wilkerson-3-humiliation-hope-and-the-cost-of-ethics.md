Dear Dr. Wilkerson,

**Subject: “Humiliation, Hope, and the Cost of Ethics”**

There are days when the emotions you feel hurt so deeply that even tears become an  
understatement, an inferior expression of the weight inside you. Today was one of those days for me, Dr. Wilkerson.

I truly hope this email finds you well and in good spirits, but **I write to you from a place of** **despair and pain.**

This is my final year, Dr. Wilkerson. The year I am meant to graduate. The year I should finally become a graduate member of the Nigerian Society of Engineers. But the challenges before me are no longer just academic. They are ethical, personal, and at moments like this, they feel impossible.

I am sorry that I always seem to write at the point where I feel powerless to do anything else. At this point, your presence, even in silence, has become a kind of pressure-release valve for me. So, even if you never read this or respond, please indulge me again today.

My day did not begin gloomy. It began as a bright Wednesday. A Wednesday I had been looking forward to for a while, but first, let me give you some context.

The final thesis topic I was given by my supervisor is on a part of Mechatronics that requires two major things, firstly, solid modelling of parts I would manufacture, and secondly, a software simulation of some electronics that would be required to run the system.

As I started to contemplate how to go about this project a few months ago, one thing was clear to me. I did not want to use pirated software for it. I did not want the last piece of original work I would do as an undergraduate student to be tainted by this plague of pirated software, so I set out to find a way to work ethically.

So, my challenge was clear. How do I use licensed software for my work? I needed two pieces of software. The solid modelling software, SolidAxis, and the simulation software, EngSim Pro. I was determined and ready to learn older versions if they were available and licensed.

As I did my research, I discovered that the student version of SolidAxis I mentioned in my first email was now available for students in Nigeria as well. Finding this was like discovering a cup of cold water in a desert. All that remained was the simulation software, EngSim Pro.

EngSim Pro had no student version, and my university did not even teach it, not to mention having an old license somewhere in the archives. At first, I felt that sinking feeling of hopelessness again until I found a Free and Open Source alternative called LibreSim.

With great enthusiasm and focus, I poured all my energy into learning LibreSim. While my course mates used pirated copies of EngSim Pro, I stayed ethical, and in little time began achieving the same or better results than them. My simulations of the control loops and actuator responses were not only more stable but also converged faster than the results produced by EngSim Pro. I was confident my supervisor would be pleased.

This morning, I was excited to present. For some reason, I was first to present. As I plugged in my laptop and began presenting my slides to a room filled with my supervisor and other undergraduate and graduate students, I noticed initial nods of affirmation. These were the little signals that told me I was on track.

Then, as I reached the slide featuring LibreSim, I saw my supervisor’s expression change. By the third LibreSim slide, he interrupted. I felt stranded, like a man lost and searching for a way out.

“Which software is this?” he asked, looking around at other students, the room was as silent as a grave yard. “Who gave you permission to use this nonsense? I thought you were a bright student. I am not sure you are ready to graduate.”

I was not sure what to say. Anything I said would have only made things worse. To call what I felt humiliation would be to call a lion’s roar a mere whisper.

For the first and last time in my undergraduate experience, I chose not to conform to the patterns I believe corrode my conscience. I decided to do things ethically, and the only result was complete failure in my seminar today.

I am not sure what hurt more. The things he said, or the fact that I allowed myself to try to do the right thing in a system like this.

Dr. Wilkerson, I honestly do not understand the pain I feel, but I now worry that I may not be able to live comfortably with this profession. Perhaps I am not really cut out to be an engineer. Perhaps.

Thank you for reading. I honestly hope I will be able to write to you again.

**Warm regards,**  
**Fola Adeoluwa**  
**Mechatronics Engineering Undergraduate Student**  
**Microris University of Technology (MUT), Lagos**
