Dear Fola,

**Subject: “Re: Humiliation, Hope, and the Cost of Ethics”**

I don’t know if this email address still works. I don’t know if you’re even checking email anymore. I know Nigerian universities have been closed since March. But I found your three letters tonight, and I’ve been sitting here for two hours not sure what I should write to you.

**I’m sorry, Fola.** I’m so, so sorry.

I was promoted to Director of the **Center for Global Engineering Education and Policy** **(CGEEP)** in January 2018, right before I came to Lagos for that conference. Your first email came about six months later, and by then I was drowning. Twelve hundred unread emails. Grant deadlines. International partnerships (with universities I had never heard of). I set up filters to manage the flood, and somehow your emails got buried in a folder I never checked.

Tonight I’m working from home because of this pandemic and I finally went through that folder. I read all three of your letters in one sitting.

Let me be honest with you in a way I couldn’t be in that keynote speech. When I stood on that stage in Lagos and talked about ethics being engineering, I meant every word. But I was speaking from a position of profound privilege. I have never had to choose between my ethics and my survival. The system I work within gives me the luxury of integrity.

You asked me: “What do you do when the ethical choice is impossible?”

I’ve been teaching engineering ethics for seventeen years, Fola. I’ve published papers on it. I’ve sat on curriculum committees and accreditation boards. And the honest answer to your question is: **I** **don’t know.** Of course, the textbooks have answers, but those answers were written by people who have likely never faced your situation.

I love Greek mythology, it sometimes helps me to find the words and language to use when situations become difficult.

The situation you’re experiencing reminds me of a word of Greek origin: ***aporia***. It means a state of being without passage, trapped in an impossible puzzle where every route forward leads to contradiction.

You’re standing in an aporia, Fola, and I helped put you there by talking about ethics as if it were universally accessible.

Your second letter about the accreditation and the lie you had to tell, that shook me.

Not because you lied, but because of what it revealed.

Your department coached you to lie. Your professors use pirated software. The accreditors suspect it, but everyone performs legitimacy because the alternative is to admit that the entire system is built on a foundation that doesn’t exist in your context.

I’ve been on accreditation teams, Fola. I’ve walked through labs that look suspiciously like yours. And you know what we do? We check the boxes. We write careful reports. And then we go home and tell ourselves that we’re maintaining standards.

But your third letter. Fola, that one broke something in me.

**You tried to do it right.** You found legal software. You learned it. You produced better work than your classmates using pirated EngSim Pro. You did everything the way we say it should be done. **And your supervisor humiliated you for it.**

Are you familiar with the myth of Procrustes?

Procrustes was an innkeeper in Greek mythology who had an iron bed. When travelers came to stay in his inn, he would make them fit the bed exactly. He would stretch them if they were too short, cut off their limbs if they were too tall.

It seems to me that your supervisor is much like Procrustes. It doesn’t seem like he cares about the quality of your work. He mostly cares that it fits the bed he recognizes, the bed that maintains the existing order.

LibreSim threatened him not because it was inferior, but because accepting it would mean acknowledging that alternatives exist. And if alternatives exist, then the whole apparatus of dependency becomes visible as a choice rather than an inevitability.

You wrote: “Perhaps I am not really cut out to be an engineer.”

No, Fola. **You are** ***exactly*** **cut out to be an engineer.** You identified a problem, researched solutions, taught yourself new tools, optimized your results, and presented your work. That’s engineering. What you’re not cut out for is the performance of compliance in a rigged system.

But here’s where I have to be honest about my own limitations. I see the problem. I can name it. But **I don’t know how to fix it.** The Center I now direct talks about “global partnerships” and “equitable access,” but our funding comes from these same corporations whose licensing models create your dilemma.

I can tell you that you’re not alone. I can tell you that your instinct to question, to resist, to try for something better, that instinct is precious and rare. The fact that it got punished doesn’t mean it was wrong.

I can also tell you something I don’t think I’ve ever written in an academic paper: The system you’re trapped in was designed. The pricing models, the licensing structures, the way “industry standard” gets defined and enforced, none of this is natural or inevitable. It serves specific interests. And those interests are not yours.

I don’t know what you decided to do after that seminar. Maybe you redid your project with EngSim Pro. Maybe you’re still in that impossible space. The pandemic has probably complicated  
everything.

But if you’re reading this, Fola: **You were right to try.** I believe your supervisor was wrong. The system that punished you is broken. And the fact that I can say that clearly while having no power to change it is its own kind of aporia. I’m trapped too, just in a much more comfortable cell. If you want to write back, I’ll read it. I can’t promise solutions, but I can promise attention. And maybe that’s where change begins. Not with grand speeches that people like me can give, but with the accumulation of individual refusals to pretend the system makes sense.

That documentation matters, Fola. Your testimony matters.

I’m sorry I wasn’t there when you needed someone to hear you. I’m here now, for whatever that’s worth.

With respect and regret,

**Patricia Wilkerson, Ph.D.**  
Associate Professor of Engineering Education  
Director, Center for Global Engineering Education and Policy  
University of Etthinghton
