Dear Dr. Wilkerson,

**Subject: “Your Response Means So Much To Me”**

I hope this email finds you well.

Your email means to me more than you may ever know. For the first time in this struggle I’ve been facing, someone saw through what I was going through and encouraged me.

This email may be shorter than my usual emails to you but I feel it is necessary that I finally respond back too.

Since I got your email some months ago, I have been delaying on writing a response. Partly because I was ashamed of myself, partly because I was tired and partly because I didn’t just know what to write. But whether I know what to write or not, someone that has been so kind to respond to me despite her busy schedule does not deserve silence from me.

You are right, Ma, COVID has indeed complicated everything. Shortly after I had that initial proposal seminar which was the context of my last email to you, I had to come to terms with reality. I couldn’t fight any longer.

**That final project work is about 6 units**. Failing it or even getting a ‘B’ would significantly affect my CGPA which could make me drop from first class. I didn’t want that. So I made a very difficult decision to just stop thinking. **I became the robot.** Followed the instructions I was given. Did what I was told.

I downloaded the pirated version of EngSim Pro, redid all of my work. When I re-presented to my supervisor again, he was raining praises on me. It was to me like comical horror but I stayed there in front of the other students and received his praises.

The funny thing is that my results with LibreSim were better than with EngSim Pro, but that didn’t matter. The results were literally almost the same except, of course, for the software interface. I’m sure I’ll get an ‘A’ for that work but it’s not something I’m proud of.

**You see why I didn’t want to write this now?** I did what I had to do to survive. But Dr. Wilkerson, **surviving feels very different from living**. I used to believe that if I just worked hard enough, was smart enough, ethical enough, I could succeed. Now I know the system doesn’t reward those things. It rewards conformity.

**But COVID wasn’t the only thing that complicated everything, Ma, the ASUU strike actually** **started before COVID and then continued even after the lock down ended.**

In March, not long after you sent your email, ASUU went on strike. Again. The Academic Staff Union of Universities. They do this almost every year, fighting for better funding, better salaries, better infrastructure. The government ignores them until they strike, then everyone suffers. Students most of all. We were sent home indefinitely.

I supported the strike. How could I not? My lecturers are paid poorly, our labs have equipment from the 1980s, and the three SolidAxis licenses I mentioned in my first email are still the only ones the department has. ASUU is fighting for the things I’ve been writing to you about. But supporting the strike meant more delays, more uncertainty about when I’d graduate.

Then in October, everything exploded.

**The EndSARS protests.** I don’t know if you heard about them in the US. Young Nigerians, mostly my age, took to the streets to protest police brutality. The Special Anti-Robbery Squad (SARS) had been killing young people for years. Extorting us. Arresting us for having laptops or nice phones, calling us criminals. We’d had enough.

I joined the protests here in Lagos. Peacefully. We marched, we chanted, we demanded change. For two weeks, it felt like maybe, finally, something could be different. Like maybe our voices mattered.

Then on October 20th, soldiers opened fire on protesters at the Lekki Toll Gate (the government said it never happened). I wasn’t there that night, thank God. But I know people who were. I know people who didn’t come back.

Five of my coursemates didn’t return to school when we were finally allowed back. I don’t know where they are. One of them, Chidi, used to sit next to me in our Control Systems class. He was at Lekki. He’s not answering his phone. We don’t know where he is. Nobody does.

Dr. Wilkerson, I keep thinking about your email. You wrote about ethics and systems and  
impossible choices. You wrote about how the system I’m trapped in was designed, that it serves specific interests, and those interests are not mine.

I’ve been thinking about that a lot. The software licensing that makes us pirates. The underfunded universities that make us desperate. The police brutality that makes us afraid. The government that shoots at us when we ask for better. These aren’t separate problems. They’re the same problem.

I feel like I’m learning to be complicit in everything. I complied with my supervisor’s demand for EngSim Pro. I stayed silent during the accreditation. And now, after Lekki, I’m back in school, head down, trying to graduate, pretending everything is normal. Because what else can I do?

We finally wrote our exams last month. I passed everything. I’ll probably graduate with first class honors in March. My parents will be so proud. I’ll be the first person in my family to graduate from university.

But I keep asking myself: What have I actually learned? How to use pirated software? How to lie when necessary? How to praise a system that punishes integrity? How to keep my head down when soldiers are shooting at people my age?

**Is this what it means to be an engineer in Nigeria? Is this what success looks like?**

Your email said my testimony matters, that documentation matters. I want to believe that. But right now, it feels like the only thing that matters is survival. And I’m not even sure I’m doing that right.

I’m sorry this email is darker than the others. I’m just tired, Ma. I’m tired of compromising. I’m tired of being afraid. I’m tired of watching my friends disappear and pretending everything is fine.

But thank you for writing. **Thank you for seeing me.** Even when I feel like I’m disappearing too.

**Warm regards,**  
**Fola Adeoluwa**  
**Mechatronics Engineering Undergraduate Student**  
**Microris University of Technology (MUT), Lagos**
